A WALK WITH JORDAN

The crosswalk

A few blocks later, Jordan comes back to the larger question. “Even if we can test arguments, religion still feels different. People say something can be true for you and not for somebody else.”

You reach a crosswalk just as a car is coming. Jordan starts forward, notices it, and stops. You ask whether the car can be there for you but not there for him. He laughs. “Okay, obviously not.”

Then he shakes his head. “But that is a car. We can both see it. We did not see Jesus rise from the dead, and people disagree about religion constantly.”

As you cross, he says, “So how do we know what is actually true when we were not there to see it ourselves?”

THE IDEA

Truth is about reality, not preference. We can know many things reasonably without having absolute certainty.

Before we can weigh evidence for God or Christianity, we have to be clear about what we mean by truth and what counts as knowing something. Otherwise, every disagreement can be dismissed by saying, “That is true for you,” which does not actually help us figure out what is true.

THE EVIDENCE

Truth, certainty, and historical knowledge

Truth is not whatever someone sincerely believes or whatever a group of people agrees to believe. A statement is true when it matches reality. A person can sincerely believe something and still be wrong.

For example, if one person says Jesus rose from the dead and another person says Jesus did not rise from the dead, both claims cannot be true in the same sense. One of them describes what actually happened and the other does not. That does not mean we automatically know which person is right, but it does mean there is a real answer to the question.

Here is another problem: people sometimes act as though we need absolute certainty before we can know anything. But that is not how we live. We know all kinds of things that we did not personally see. We rely on evidence, testimony, documents, memory, and inference every day. Testimony is simply information given to us by another person, and it can be good evidence when that person is in a position to know what happened and has reason to be trusted.

Justification is the reason we have for believing something is true. The stronger the evidence, the stronger our justification can be. Certainty is different. You may not be able to prove a historical claim in an absolute sense, but that does not mean you have no good reason to believe it.

Given this, Christianity does not need to be proven beyond every possible doubt before it can reasonably be believed. The better question is whether the evidence is strong enough to justify belief. Maybe not beyond all doubt, but beyond reasonable doubt. That is the standard we will use as we look at the evidence for God and Christianity.

Bottom line: Truth depends on reality, and we can reasonably know something without being absolutely certain about it.

Think about it: Why can testimony count as evidence even when you did not see the event yourself?
Answer: Because we can reasonably learn from people who were in a position to know what happened and who have reason to be trusted. We use testimony all the time. The question is not whether it is testimony, but how good the testimony is.
BACK ON THE WALK

Truth is about reality, and evidence can justify belief

You explain that belief and truth are not the same thing. People can sincerely disagree, but contradictory claims cannot both match reality in the same sense.

You also point out that most historical knowledge does not come from personally seeing the event. We use testimony, documents, physical evidence, memory, and inference all the time.

Jordan says, “That makes more sense than saying I have to see everything myself. I still want to know how good the evidence is, though.”

COMMON OBJECTIONS

Questions this argument still has to face

“That may be true for you.”

If someone says something is “true for you,” first ask whether they are talking about a preference or about reality. Two people may be equally sincere about whether Jesus rose from the dead, but contradictory claims cannot both describe what actually happened.

“You were not there, so you cannot know.”

That is true, none of us was there. But none of us is personally present for most of the historical events we believe happened. We know history through testimony, documents, and other evidence. The real question is whether the evidence is strong enough to justify the conclusion.

KEEP THE CLAIM CLEAR

Do not claim more certainty than the evidence gives. The strength of our belief should match the strength of the evidence.
