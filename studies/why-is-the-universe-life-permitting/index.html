Why is the universe life-permitting?

The universe has the physical conditions needed for stars, chemistry, and life. Why do those conditions fall in the right ranges?

THE THREAD

Last time, Jordan asked for something more specific than saying nature looks complicated. Now we look at the universe's basic physical settings and ask why they permit life at all.

A WALK WITH JORDAN

The stars overhead

The next walk runs later than usual. As you turn back through the park, the first stars are coming out. Jordan looks up. “Last time you said we needed something more specific than ‘nature looks designed.’ Is this where you're going?”

You point toward the stars. Before any planet could support life, you tell him, the universe needed conditions that allow stars, atoms, and complex chemistry in the first place.

“But isn't that just because we're here?” Jordan asks. “If those conditions were different, there wouldn't be anybody around to complain.”

“That explains why we can notice the conditions,” you say. “Does it explain why they're there?” Jordan looks back up. “All right. Show me what's so unusual about them, and then tell me why you think God explains it.”

THE IDEA

The universe's basic conditions permit life within remarkably restricted ranges. In light of the earlier reasons for believing in a rational Creator, intentional design offers a positive explanation for why those conditions exist.

Fine-tuning gives us a specific feature of the universe to explain: its basic physical conditions permit stable matter, stars, complex chemistry, and life. We will ask why that fact fits with intentional creation, not just list possible answers.

THE EVIDENCE

Why these conditions point toward a Designer

Before life could exist on any planet, the universe needed atoms that hold together, stars that last long enough to provide energy, and chemistry capable of forming complex structures. All of that depends on the universe's basic laws, physical constants, and starting conditions. Physicists call this fine-tuning because, in the models they study, changing some of those values would make a universe capable of supporting complex life impossible.

Consider the cosmological constant, which is connected to the expansion of the universe. In models where the other conditions are held fixed, a much larger positive value would prevent matter from gathering into galaxies. Other examples involve the forces that govern stars and the production of heavier elements. We are not saying every part of the universe is comfortable for life. The point is that the universe has the underlying conditions needed for life to exist anywhere.

Why is that remarkable? Think of several settings that all need to fall within particular ranges. In the ranges physicists can investigate, much of the available space is not life-permitting. Yet the conditions of our universe work together. If this is the only universe, and its basic settings were established without any purpose directing them toward life, that is a striking outcome. We cannot give it an exact one-in-so-many probability, because we do not know how possible values would be distributed. Still, the narrow life-permitting ranges are real features of the physical models, and they deserve an explanation.

One suggestion is physical necessity: perhaps the constants could not have been different. That would be important if it could be established, but we do not currently have a demonstrated theory showing that all the relevant values had to be life-permitting. Another suggestion is chance. But chance is not a force that sets the universe's constants. It describes the likelihood of an outcome. We would still need to identify the physical process that produced those settings.

The multiverse proposal takes that question in a different direction. If there really are many universes with sufficiently varied physical conditions, some might permit life. Any observers would naturally find themselves in one of those. That would make our observation less surprising under that proposal. But the additional universes have not been directly observed. Some multiverse ideas arise from theoretical physics, yet a useful explanation still needs good reasons to believe such a collection exists and that its conditions vary in the way the argument requires. Simply imagining enough universes is not evidence that they are there.

Now consider design. In earlier studies, we examined reasons to believe that physical reality depends on a rational Creator. If that Creator intended a universe capable of supporting living beings, then the life-permitting conditions are exactly the kind of conditions we would expect Him to establish. Purposeful intelligence gives us a positive reason for the universe to have this kind of structure. On a single-universe account with no design or necessity, we are left saying that the settings happened to work out. Design gives us an explanation for why they work out toward a particular end.

There is also a difference between how scientists investigate the universe and what ultimately explains its existence. Physics ordinarily studies natural processes. That is a useful method; it does not, by itself, settle the philosophical question of whether a Creator established the laws those processes follow. We do not need to assume that scientists personally reject God, or rule out a Creator before considering the evidence.

Given the case we have already been building, I think intentional design explains the life-permitting structure of our universe better than an unexplained, one-off outcome. The argument is not that we have found a gap in physics and inserted God. It is that the physical structure we do know about fits what we would expect from a rational Creator who intended life to be possible.

Bottom line: The universe has basic physical conditions that allow galaxies, stars, complex chemistry, and life. Chance names an outcome, not a cause; physical necessity has not been established for every relevant setting; and a multiverse would need a supported mechanism producing universes with the right variety. A rational Creator who intended life, on the other hand, has a reason to establish life-permitting conditions. Given the evidence for a Creator already discussed in this course, I think intentional design offers the stronger explanation for why our universe can support life.

Think about it: Why does the design argument explain more than simply saying the universe got lucky?
Answer: A rational Creator who intended living beings has a reason to establish life-permitting conditions. That is a positive explanation that also fits the earlier arguments for a Creator.
BACK ON THE WALK

Why design belongs in the explanation

“So the cosmological constant is one of the things you'd point to?” Jordan asks. “If it were different, there might not even be galaxies?”

“That's right. And it's one example of how much the universe depends on its basic settings.”

Jordan shrugs. “Maybe we got lucky. Or maybe there are a lot of other universes, and this is the one that worked.”

“Possibly, if those other universes really exist and their conditions vary enough. But luck doesn't set the constants, and we haven't observed another universe. We'd still need to show what produces them.”

“So why do you think a Creator explains it better?”

“Because we've already looked at reasons to believe there is a rational Creator. If He intended to make a world where life could exist, these are just the conditions He would need to establish. That gives us a reason for the result instead of simply saying it happened.”

Jordan looks up again. “I want to check those examples for myself.” You nod. “You should.”

COMMON OBJECTIONS

Questions this argument still has to face

“Maybe we just got lucky.”

That describes the outcome, but it does not identify a cause. In a single undesigned universe, life depends on several physical conditions being within restricted ranges. We cannot claim a precise numerical probability, but we can still ask why the conditions are life-permitting.

“There could be countless other universes.”

A sufficiently large and varied multiverse could explain why at least one universe permits observers. But we have not directly observed other universes. Some multiverse models have motivations in theoretical physics; the question is whether a particular model has evidence behind it and actually produces the needed variety of conditions.

“Maybe the constants had to be this way.”

That is what physical necessity would mean. If a well-supported theory established that the constants must have life-permitting values, it would change the discussion. We do not presently have that demonstration for all the relevant conditions.

“A Designer is not a scientific explanation.”

Physics investigates natural mechanisms. The question here goes further: why does the physical order have life-permitting conditions at all? Using natural explanations in science does not establish that a rational Creator could not have intended that order. We have already considered independent reasons for believing in such a Creator.

CONVERSATION TIPS

Use this in a real conversation

  • Start with a concrete example. Explain what a larger positive cosmological constant would do to galaxy formation before talking about design.
  • Do not claim a one-in-so-many probability unless you can explain where the calculation and its assumptions come from. The life-permitting ranges are enough to begin the conversation.
  • If someone brings up a multiverse, ask what physical theory supports that particular proposal, whether it yields varied constants, and what evidence exists for it.
  • Then give the positive reason for design: if a rational Creator intended living beings, life-permitting conditions make sense. Connect that point to the arguments already made rather than treating design as a last resort.
  • Physics has good reasons to study natural mechanisms. That approach does not establish that God does not exist, and it does not tell us what any individual scientist believes.
KEEP THE CLAIM CLEAR

Use the actual sensitivity of the physical conditions, not a dramatic probability figure you cannot defend. A multiverse has not been directly observed, but some versions have theoretical motivations. The positive claim is that, alongside the earlier arguments for a rational Creator, design explains why the universe is capable of supporting life.
