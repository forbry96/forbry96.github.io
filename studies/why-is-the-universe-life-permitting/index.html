Why is the universe life-permitting?

The universe has the conditions life needs. Why do those conditions work out?

THE THREAD

Last time, Jordan asked for something more specific than saying nature looks complicated. Now we look at the universe's basic physical settings and ask why they permit life at all.

A WALK WITH JORDAN

The stars overhead

The next walk runs later than usual. As you turn back through the park, the first stars are coming out. Jordan looks up. “Last time you said we needed something more specific than ‘nature looks designed.’ Is this where you're going?”

You point toward the stars. Before any planet could support life, you tell him, the universe needed conditions that allow stars, atoms, and complex chemistry in the first place.

“But isn't that just because we're here?” Jordan asks. “If those conditions were different, there wouldn't be anybody around to complain.”

“That explains why we can notice the conditions,” you say. “Does it explain why they're there?” Jordan looks back up. “All right. Show me what's so unusual about them, and then tell me why you think God explains it.”

THE IDEA

The universe has the right conditions for life to exist. If God intended to create a universe capable of supporting life, that gives us a reason why those conditions are there in the first place.

Before life could exist on any planet, the universe needed the right conditions for stars, atoms, and chemistry to exist at all. Some of those conditions could not change very much before life would be impossible. Why do they fall within the ranges life needs?

THE EVIDENCE

Why do the conditions for life point toward a Designer?

Before life could exist on any planet, the universe needed atoms that hold together, stars that last, and the right conditions for complex chemistry. These things depend on the universe's basic settings. Scientists call this fine-tuning: change some of those settings enough, and the universe could no longer support life.

Take the cosmological constant. This is a number in physics connected to the expansion of the universe. If it were much larger and positive, while other conditions stayed the same, space would expand in a way that prevents matter from gathering into galaxies. Other settings affect whether stars can last long enough and whether the elements needed for life can form. So the question is not whether every corner of the universe is friendly to life. It is why the universe allows life to exist anywhere.

When scientists change these settings in their models, many of the results could not support life. Some values fall within a narrow range that allows stars or complex chemistry to exist. Yet the settings of our universe work together. If this is the only universe and nobody intended it to support life, that is a remarkable outcome. We cannot honestly attach an exact one-in-so-many chance to it, because we do not know how likely each possible set of settings would be. But we can see that the conditions life depends on are not guaranteed by the models we have.

Could the settings have been unavoidable? Perhaps the laws of physics required them to be exactly what they are. That is a possibility, but no established theory shows that every important setting had to allow life. What about chance? Chance describes how likely something is; it is not a force that sets the values. We would still need to explain how the universe came to have them.

Someone might suggest that there are many universes with different settings. If enough of them exist, some could support life, and naturally we would find ourselves in one of those. But we have not directly observed any other universes. Some ideas in physics give scientists reasons to explore a multiverse, but we still need evidence that it exists and that its universes differ in the ways this explanation requires. Saying there might be countless universes is not the same as showing that there are.

Now consider what we have already learned. Earlier studies gave us reasons to believe the universe depends on a rational Creator. If God intended to create living beings, He had a reason to give the universe the conditions life needs. That is the positive case for design. It explains why these settings would be there, rather than simply saying they happened to work out.

Science normally studies how things work through natural processes. That is valuable work. But studying those processes does not settle whether God created the universe and established the laws they follow. We do not need to guess what individual scientists believe to make the argument.

I think the conditions needed for life make better sense alongside the evidence for a Creator we have already considered. We are not putting God into something scientists have failed to explain. We are asking which explanation makes better sense of the conditions we actually find, and purposeful creation gives us a reason for them.

Bottom line: The universe has the conditions needed for stars, chemistry, and life. Some of those conditions could not change very much before life would be impossible. Calling it luck does not explain what produced those settings, and no other universes have been directly observed. We have already looked at reasons to believe in a rational Creator. If God intended life to exist, He had a reason to make a universe that could support it. That is why I think design makes better sense of the evidence than simply saying the conditions happened to work out.

Think about it: Why is design more than saying the universe happened to get lucky?
Answer: If a rational Creator intended life to exist, He had a reason to make a universe with the conditions life needs. That also fits the arguments for a Creator we looked at earlier.
BACK ON THE WALK

Why design belongs in the explanation

“So the cosmological constant is one of the things you'd point to?” Jordan asks. “If it were different, there might not even be galaxies?”

“That's right. And it's one example of how much the universe depends on its basic settings.”

Jordan shrugs. “Maybe we got lucky. Or maybe there are a lot of other universes, and this is the one that worked.”

“Possibly, if those other universes really exist and their conditions vary enough. But luck doesn't set the constants, and we haven't observed another universe. We'd still need to show what produces them.”

“So why do you think a Creator explains it better?”

“Because we've already looked at reasons to believe there is a rational Creator. If He intended to make a world where life could exist, these are just the conditions He would need to establish. That gives us a reason for the result instead of simply saying it happened.”

Jordan looks up again. “I want to check those examples for myself.” You nod. “You should.”

COMMON OBJECTIONS

Questions this argument still has to face

“Maybe we just got lucky.”

Maybe the settings happened to work out, but luck does not tell us what caused them. Some settings could not change very much before life would be impossible. We do not know the exact odds, but that is still worth explaining.

“There could be countless other universes.”

If many universes exist with different settings, some might support life. But we have not directly observed any others. Some theories in physics give reasons to explore the idea. We would still need evidence for a multiverse that actually produces the differences needed to explain life.

“Maybe the constants had to be this way.”

That is possible. If scientists established that all these values had to allow life, it would change the argument. We do not currently have a theory showing that every relevant value had to be what it is.

“A Designer is not a scientific explanation.”

Science usually investigates natural processes. The question here is why the universe has laws and settings that allow life in the first place. Studying natural processes does not establish that a Creator could not have intended them. We have already considered other reasons to believe a Creator exists.

CONVERSATION TIPS

Use this in a real conversation

  • Start with an example. Explain how a much larger positive cosmological constant would prevent galaxies from forming, then show why that matters for life.
  • Do not claim exact odds that you cannot back up. The important point is that some basic settings could not change much before life would be impossible.
  • If someone brings up a multiverse, ask what evidence supports it and whether the proposed universes really would have different settings.
  • Make the positive case for design. If a rational Creator intended life, He had a reason to establish the conditions life needs. Connect this to the earlier studies.
  • Science looks for natural explanations of how things work. That does not settle whether God created the universe.
KEEP THE CLAIM CLEAR

Explain what would happen if particular settings changed, but do not give exact odds you cannot support. Other universes have not been directly observed, though some scientific theories lead people to explore that possibility. The argument for design builds on the reasons for a Creator already covered in this course.
