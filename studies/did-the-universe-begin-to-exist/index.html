Did the universe begin to exist?

The Kalam asks a simple question: if the universe began to exist, what caused it?

THE THREAD

Contingency asks why reality exists. Kalam asks a different question: did the universe itself have a beginning?

A WALK WITH JORDAN

Then what about the universe?

Jordan does not let the last point sit for long. “Suppose I grant that dependent things need some kind of ultimate explanation. Why cannot the universe itself be that thing?”

You tell him that there is another question to ask: whether the universe has always existed or whether physical reality had a beginning.

Jordan immediately says, “This is where Christians lose me. I have heard people say everything needs a cause, and then as soon as somebody asks who caused God, suddenly God does not need one.”

He shakes his head. “What is the actual argument? What exactly are you saying needs a cause?”

THE IDEA

Whatever begins to exist has a cause. The universe began to exist. Therefore, the universe has a cause beyond itself.

The Kalam argument is easy to understand, but that does not mean we should rush through it. The argument is only as good as its premises, so we need to ask whether things that begin need causes, whether the universe began, and then what kind of cause could explain the beginning of the physical universe.

THE EVIDENCE

Defend the two premises, then ask what follows

The Kalam cosmological argument is very simple to state:

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause. 2. The universe began to exist. 3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

The first premise is not saying that everything needs a cause. It is saying that when something begins to exist, there needs to be some explanation for why it came into being. If things could simply appear from nothing without a cause, then it becomes difficult to explain why we do not see things appearing uncaused all the time. Additionally, our ordinary experience strongly supports the idea that beginnings have causes.

The first premise tells us that things which begin to exist need a cause. But that only gets us somewhere if the universe itself began to exist. That is what the second premise claims, and it is where more of the debate happens. There are philosophical arguments against an actually infinite past, because a beginningless series of past events creates serious problems about how an infinite sequence could ever be completed one event at a time. There is also scientific evidence. The Big Bang theory describes the observable universe expanding from a much hotter and denser earlier state. This is very different from the older idea of a static universe that had simply existed forever.

Another piece of evidence for the second premise is the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin (BGV) theorem. In simple terms, it shows that if the universe has been expanding on average, that expansion cannot be traced backward forever under the same conditions. Scientists call this “past-incomplete,” meaning there is a limit to how far back that description of the universe can take us. That does not tell us exactly what happened beyond that limit, and it does not prove creation from nothing by itself. The Kalam does not depend on this one theorem.

If the evidence is sufficient to establish both premises, then the conclusion follows: the universe has a cause. The conclusion gets us to a cause. From there, we can ask what kind of cause could bring the universe into existence? If space, time, matter, and energy all began with the universe, its ultimate cause could not simply be another physical thing within it or something that existed earlier in physical time. It would have to be transcendent, meaning beyond the physical universe it caused, and powerful enough to bring that universe into existence.

If the cause of the universe also began to exist, then it would need a cause of its own. But when we are talking about the ultimate cause, we are looking for something that did not itself begin to exist. In other words, something eternal. A cause that is beyond physical reality, eternal, and powerful enough to bring the universe into existence fits with what Christians mean when we speak of God as Creator. This adds another important part to the case for a Creator.

Bottom line: If the universe began to exist, it needs a cause beyond the physical universe itself.

Think about it: Why use both philosophical and scientific support for the second premise?
Answer: They are independent lines of reasoning. The philosophical argument concerns an infinite past; the scientific evidence concerns the history of the physical universe.
BACK ON THE WALK

A beginning requires a cause beyond the physical beginning

You clarify the part Jordan had heard misstated: the Kalam does not say everything has a cause. It says whatever begins to exist has a cause.

If physical space, time, matter, and energy began with the universe, then the ultimate cause cannot simply be another physical event sitting earlier inside the same spacetime.

Jordan nods. “Okay. If the universe really began, then something beyond the physical universe caused it. I can see that. That sounds a lot closer to a Creator than I expected.”

He looks around the park. “But what makes you think that cause is anything like a mind?”

COMMON OBJECTIONS

Questions this argument still has to face

“Maybe things really can come from nothing.”

When people point to quantum physics, the important distinction is that quantum events do not come from literal nothing. They occur within physical reality, with fields, laws, and an existing system already in place. The Kalam is asking whether something can begin to exist with no cause and no prior reality at all.

“The Big Bang does not prove God.”

It does not, and the Kalam does not need the Big Bang to prove God. The scientific evidence can support the premise that the universe has a finite history, while philosophical arguments provide another line of support. The conclusion about a cause comes only after those premises are defended.

CONVERSATION TIPS

Use this in a real conversation

  • Cosmology can get technical very quickly. Separate the philosophical argument from whichever scientific model happens to be under discussion.
  • A person may know a cosmological model you have never studied. It is better to say that and ask how the model answers the specific question about a beginning than to bluff your way through physics.
  • The premise you are defending is that whatever begins to exist has a cause, not that everything has a cause.
KEEP THE CLAIM CLEAR

The Kalam gives a causal argument for the universe's beginning. The philosophical arguments and scientific evidence help us assess its second premise, but the conclusion follows from both premises together.
